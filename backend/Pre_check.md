## 👋 My story

- **My experience with hackathons**
    
    I failed 7 hackathons in a row. Not even a single honorable mention. Just... pure back-to-back losses.
    
    But on my **8th try**, something finally clicked.
    
    And, we **won the first place**.
    
    In this guide, I’m gonna walk you through the 7 lessons I learned from each of fails - plus a bonus one that made all the difference in the end.
    
    Whether you’re prepping for your _first hackathon_, or still chasing that _first win_, this might help you skip a few of my mistakes.
    
    > If you’re not coming from YouTube to find this guide, you can also watch the video version of it:
    
    ![YouTube video](https://youtu.be/M7ethQsjhlQ?si=4PWx4ddCm00P8lAC)

## 🫂 Lesson 1

- **Team > Randoms**
    
    In my first few hackathons, I used to join **solo**. I’d show up, find random people at the event, and try to build something in 24–48 hours with complete strangers.
    
    What actually happened? Everyone had different ideas & different coding styles. It was awkward & very slow. Half the time was spent just figuring out who does what.
    
    When I finally started teaming up before the hackathon, everything got easier. We already knew each other’s strengths, discussed ideas in advance, and had a shared mindset.
    
    The lesson? Find people you vibe with. Build trust early. Enter as a team. It can make a huge difference.
    
    And yeah, if you’ve ever solo-queued in a competitive game… you know the struggle.
    

## 🦄 Lesson 2

- **Choose diverse teammates**
    
    _"But, what if it's my first time or I don't have a team yet?"_ you might ask. Well, in that case, you need to pick your teammates wisely.
    
    I used to team up with other random programmers like me. But there was _no one_ in the team for pitching, design, or business planning.
    
    The thing is your teammates shouldn't be clones of you. You need to always look for complementary skills. Ideally, you want:
    
    - One person good with design or UX
    - One frontend dev
    - One backend dev
    - One business-focused person who can pitch
    - And, one well-rounded person (he's often the leader)
    
    But here’s the thing: **don’t ignore random teammates altogether**.
    
    In fact, in the hackathon we won, half of our team were randoms we met on Discord. And it worked really well. We clicked fast, communicated well, and had a lot of fun.
    
    So yes, teaming up beforehand helps—but if you’re open, even randoms can turn into a strong team.
    

## 🧱 Lesson 3

- **Pick buildable ideas, not fancy ones**
    
    One of my biggest mistakes early on was chasing the most flashy & unrealistic ideas just to impress the judges.
    
    I still remember pitching this:
    
    > _What if we made asphalt roads that generate electricity from cars driving over them & it melts snow in winter, and blows away leaves in autumn to keep the road clean?_
    
    Yes. I actually said that.
    
    Do I regret it? **Of course**. Did it teach me something? **Definitely**.
    
    Hackathons aren’t about solving climate change in 1 day. They’re about building something that works.
    
    A good hackathon idea:
    
    - Is simple & can be understood by an average person _(although some hackathons can be challenging like **CTF**)_
    - Solves a real, relatable problem _(although challenges are often provided by organizers)_
    - Is possible to design, build, and present in under 48 hours.
    
    Let go of impressing judges with buzzwords. While it might sound impressive, you ideally want to build something simple, clear, and _**demoable**_. Is that even a real word?
    

## 🎯 Lesson 4

- **Focus on what really matters**
    
    Now, you have a team & an idea. Next part is knowing what to work on. This took me quite a lot of painful hackathons to understand.
    
    Judges don’t care about how much code you wrote or how little you slept.
    
    They care about:
    
    - What problem did you solve?
    - Why does it matter?
    - How usable or impactful is your solution?
    
    And, I repeat once again: _**Judges don't care about how much code you wrote.**_
    
    We often used to waste time on features no one cares or obsess over some backend API logic that no one sees.
    
    But when the deadline was a few hours away, we wouldn't have enough time for preparing the pitch.
    
    Think like this: Judges have **120 seconds** & **10 slides** to understand your project. That's **12 seconds per slide**. What would you show them? That's what matters.
    
    Balance the technical execution with presentation side. But, how do you do it?
    

## **🏃‍♂️ Lesson 5**

- **Plan the sprint, not the marathon**
    
    You only have 24 to 48 hours at a hackathon. That’s a sprint, not a full startup project. And, in a sprint, every second counts.
    
    In the beginning, we'd get all hyped about the idea, plan a huge app with multiple screens, user auth, clean dashboards, cool-looking charts & so on, but at the end, we'd barely finish _one-third_ of it.
    
    Eventually, I learned to cut **ruthlessly**. In every hackathon, I now ask these questions:
    
    - Okay, what's the **ONE** core feature we need to build that shows the concept works?
    - What's the **bare minimum** we need for the demo? (that's **MVP**)
    - And, who's doing what in the next few hours?
    
    That **mindset shift** - from perfection to progress - helped us focus on **building the core idea first** and leave room to **polish it later.**
    

## **🔄️ Lesson 6**

- **Templates save time**
    
    By my 6th or 7th hackathon, I started to notice a pattern - we were doing the same stuff over and over.
    
    Slides, project & pitch outlines, even the way we explained the problem (such as market size & metrics)… it was always the same structure, just different ideas.
    
    So, I built templates. Since I often handle design or frontend role in a team, these saved tons of time.
    
- **BONUS: Pitch template**
    
    What’s a **pitch**?
    
    As quoted by experienced mentors, **pitch** is about telling the story and making people (aka audience) _interested in_ the stuff you’re making.
    
    The pitch in hackathons usually lasts between **2-3 minutes**. And, you want to make sure you squeeze every second out of it. But, how do you do it?
    
    The **golden rule** of every successful pitch is:
    
    - Start with **WHY?** (the problem)
    - Explain **HOW?** (the market)
    - And, end with **WHAT?** (your product/solution)
    
    As we saw earlier, you’re given maximum **180 seconds** to pitch the project idea you & your team have been working for the last **48 hours**.
    
    Here’s a **general pitch outline** I follow & average **time to spend** on each part (this outline is also recommended by mentors):
    
    - **(10 secs)**: OPENING - Title & Company name
    - **(30 secs)**: (Problem - Why?
    - **(30 secs)**: Solution - How?
    - **(20 secs)**: Product demo/Prototype - What?
    - **(25 secs)**: Competition/Inspiration + USP - How you are different?
    - **(20 secs)**: Market size - Business model?
    - **(15 secs)**: Validation - Traction - What’s done?
    - **(10 secs)**: Team - Who?
    - **(10 secs)**: Next steps and projection (funding, etc.)
    - **(10 secs)**: CLOSING - Vision (Thank you/Your questions)
    
    Before we go to slides part, let’s make sure your pitch is 100% ready.
    
    If a judge asks you to explain your hackathon project in **1 sentence**, how would you do it?
    
    Well, don’t worry. There’s already a system we can follow. It’s called _value proposition canvas_. It looks like this:
    
    ![Value proposition](https://file.notion.so/f/f/edef5176-fdfe-4281-898c-6b19db7f132c/bd3e9fee-67b8-4d46-b9aa-270e06bf7925/b2b-value-proposition-template-example.webp?table=block&id=1d5b2f26-d7bb-80b2-9ff6-efae2825c087&spaceId=edef5176-fdfe-4281-898c-6b19db7f132c&expirationTimestamp=1745078400000&signature=xYyC4FuFSkLUwmYvmOn0EPWu4i3NRrJRgZ-S_oaaPEE&downloadName=b2b-value-proposition-template-example.webp)
    
    Here’s a simpler format:
    
    > **Our** [product/solution] **helps** [target audience] **who** [struggle with/problem] **by** [solution] **and** [benefit to customer]. (**unlike** [competitors])
    
    Basically, all you need to do is to fill out the blank spaces in the sentence. In AirBnB example, it would look something like this:
    
    > Our platform helps budget-conscious travelers who struggle to find affordable and local accommodations by connecting them with hosts offering spare rooms. (unlike [hotels.com](http://hotels.com) & [rentobi.com](http://rentobi.com))
    
    Be ready to describe your hackathon project in just **1 sentence**.

    Don’t forget to use the _**value proposition canvas**_ here as we looked at in the **BONUS: Pitch template**.
    
    > **Our** [product/solution] **helps** [target audience] **who** [struggle with/problem] **by** [solution] **and** [benefit to customer]. (**unlike** [competitors])

    As the title suggests, this section is for showcasing your product, either 3D prototype or web platform. Just make sure to show the **core value** of your product here, not the sign-up or welcome pages. Often times, you’re required to submit your slides in `PDF` format, which means you can’t use animations to make them look cooler. (I also wish it was possible xD).

    ### 6. Market size - Business model
    
    These next 2 slides tend to be **the most important slides** of your pitch. It tells the judges or potential investors about how you’re going to **make money** from the idea.
    
    ![image.png](https://file.notion.so/f/f/edef5176-fdfe-4281-898c-6b19db7f132c/8ed2c2ff-e699-40c1-a5e7-9c46ca7e95bd/image.png?table=block&id=1d7b2f26-d7bb-80de-bd13-c264400bcf95&spaceId=edef5176-fdfe-4281-898c-6b19db7f132c&expirationTimestamp=1745078400000&signature=fubgS1_B-ZcagVl1G6RgBU6cB1dLE7TmOw45ZUxJJNU&downloadName=image.png)
    
    > 💡 **Tip**: Don’t use random numbers, like **1.9 billion** or **532 million**. You will need to research them first (_Google it_) to understand whether people would _really_ need/buy your solution. Also, in **Business Model** _(slide below)_, first observe how the competitors are making money and how much do they charge for their customers. Based on that info, you can estimate your average revenue.

    ### 7. Validation - Traction - What’s done?
    
    Although validation is **not often required** to do in hackathons, it can give your team an advantage when you go an extra mile. Still, if the other parts of your pitch, like market size and demo, are weak, it’s far better to work on them rather adding yet another slide to your pitch. Only work on this slide if your team is **100% sure** that other slides are fully complete.

    ### 8. Team - Who?
    
    While team section is not shown in the original AirBnB pitch slide, it’s still an important part of your slides. Even often times, in **Judging Criteria**, judges **care about** how well your team is coordinated & whether you’ve a diverse set of skills to further develop the idea. That’s why I emphasized more on the **team selection** in the first 2 lessons.
    
    > 💡 **Tip**: In this slide, you can either use standalone photos as shown below or take a group photo during the hackathon and put as one big photo. None of them makes a massive difference, but I would **favor** the team photo **60%-70%** of the time.
    
    ![image.png](https://file.notion.so/f/f/edef5176-fdfe-4281-898c-6b19db7f132c/1b8b7383-a741-49cc-9ec0-223e1d9416fe/image.png?table=block&id=1d8b2f26-d7bb-80fd-bba5-eeef7af932cb&spaceId=edef5176-fdfe-4281-898c-6b19db7f132c&expirationTimestamp=1745078400000&signature=-PPUGSbJRLyx_eLRGNTcuwnH9LZEtx5-ppQBDc7PBQM&downloadName=image.png)
    
    ### 9. Next steps and projection (funding, etc.)
    
    The biggest mistake we used to make in this slide was just put generic terms, like:
    
    - We’ll launch the demo app in 3 months
    - We’ll do marketing campaign to promote the app
    - We’ll test our demo app
    
    The big fault with this technique is that they’re not specific. Because everybody, especially judges, knows you’ll do marketing campaign, launch the demo & other things. So, it would a waste of slide & time to mention them.
    
    Instead you need to **narrow it down**. In AirBnB case, they go straight to the point by asking how much money they need to develop their project & make revenue out of it. It works really well because it tells the judges/investors **a clear picture** of what you’ll do with your team after **this hackathon** **ends**.
    
    > 💡 **Tip**: One giveaway lesson from this slide is “The **more specific** you’re, the **better you do** in a hackathon.”. Avoid using generic terms & sentences. Because that’s what other teams do. And, you want to stand out from them. You need to tell the judges that you actually **did** your homework & did **go an extra mile** in this hackathon.
    
    ![image.png](https://file.notion.so/f/f/edef5176-fdfe-4281-898c-6b19db7f132c/fe917bee-d439-4483-9b42-a8863564a846/image.png?table=block&id=1d8b2f26-d7bb-8043-b5dd-fd193fc90296&spaceId=edef5176-fdfe-4281-898c-6b19db7f132c&expirationTimestamp=1745078400000&signature=dlBW11hvHpDrpoK9DTySlbLm-ujDJoueCk5RB3TRO6E&downloadName=image.png)
    
    ### 10. Closing - Vision (Thank you/Your questions)
    
    You can either end the slide by keeping it simple:
    
    - “Thank you. Do you have any questions?”
    
    Or end with a **vision**. Something like:
    
    - Ready to change the way people communicate?
    - Ready to give your old toys a second life?
    
    However, my favorite thing to do in this slide is put the **QR code** of the demo or prototype of the project. Because more people are _more likely_ to scan it to find out, which means _more interest_ towards our idea.
    
    Yet, it’s not absolutely _necessary_. Sometimes, you need to balance the complexity with simplicity. **Keep it simple, but not generic.**

## **📈 Lesson 7**

- **Failures are practice rounds**
    
    Hackathons are intense. You pour all your energy & sweat in the idea you love and then you don't win. Again…
    
    That was me. For 7 hackathons, _straight_.
    
    It's easy to take it personally or blame it on judges. But, I learned to take my fails as lessons. Each fail taught me something:
    
    - How to find a good idea
    - How to coordinate a team
    - How to scope a project realistically
    - How to pitch better
    - and so much more…
    
    So, if you're watching this after failing your _1st_ or _11th_ _hackathon_, that means you are still trying & don't like giving up easily. And, I like that mindset. That's the mindset that eventually wins.
    

## **🎁 Bonus Lesson**

- **Just have fun**
    
    After 7 fails, you'd think I'd quit. But I kept signing up for more hackathons.
    
    Not because I expected to win—but because of **FREE FOOD** _(just kidding)_ but because I started to _enjoy_ it.
    
    You’re surrounded by creative people, building cool things in a very short time. That’s already quite rare & impressive. Learn to embrace it & enjoy the process.
    
    Some of my **best** memories were from the hackathons I've **lost**.
    
    So, yes - plan well, pick a solid idea, build & pitch. But also?
    
    - Laugh with your team.
    - Celebrate the small wins.
    - And, most importantly, eat all that FREE FOOD.
    
    Don't let the pressure kill your joy.
    
    And, hey, at the end of the day..., it's night.
    
    See you at the next hackathon. Hopefully not on the enemy team.
